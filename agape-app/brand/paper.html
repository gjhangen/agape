Food to the seat is how Agape gets in. This paper sets out what that position makes possible, how big each line gets, who has already proved each one, the order of moves, and what could break it.

Prepared for investors · ConfidentialNovember 2026

Agape's real asset is not food delivery. It is the position food delivery puts it in: inside the fan's phone at the moment they are spending, on the money path between fans and every business in and around the venue, and holding the identity that ties one night to the next. Every other line of business in this paper is that position, put to work.

1The thesis

Food to the seat is the wedge because it is the one thing every fan at every event already buys, and the line is the one thing every fan already hates. Solving it earns four things no single competitor holds together:

  1. The moment. Fans open Agape while they are at the event, attentive and spending. Sponsors and nearby businesses pay most for exactly that moment.
  2. The money path. Fans pay Agape, and Agape pays the stands, runners and tax. Whoever sits on the money path sees every transaction and can add services to it.
  3. The merchant relationships. Every stand, venue and nearby business Agape works with becomes a relationship Agape can sell more to: payments, marketing, payouts, data.
  4. The fan identity. One account, a level and a history that follows the fan to every venue. Teams, artists, sponsors and leagues today each hold a slice of the fan; Agape can hold the whole fan.

Each of these is worth more the more of the others Agape has. That is why the opportunity is a web, not a ladder: every line below draws on several of the four and strengthens several in return.

The north star30% of all money spent around live events flowing through Agape, and the businesses around every venue running their everyday card volume through Agape as well.

No one else at a venue holds the first three together. Ticketing companies know who the fan is but not what they spend that night. The concessionaire and its register see the spending but not the fan. Local businesses have neither. The payments side has noticed: Shift4, a card processor, bought VenueNext in 2021 and SpotOn's stadium business for $100M in 2023 to get into venue commerce. The money path at venues is worth paying for. But Shift4 came in through the register. Agape comes in through the fan, and the fan is the one asset a register can't buy.

3The web

Read it as a web, not a staircase. Food to the seat is the only way in, but once Agape is in the seat, several lines leave it at the same time, and they feed each other.

The local line: food → local deals → processing → advertising. This is the line that turns an events company into a payments company. Fans at the event get offers from bars, restaurants and shops nearby. The "What Agape brought them" report shows each business exactly what that sent it. With that proof in hand, Agape asks for two things a business already pays for: card processing (about 1.6% of every card sale goes to processing fees in the US) and marketing (US local businesses spend about $169B a year on advertising). Processing margin alone is a fraction of a percent; processing bundled with the marketing budget is how "1% of revenue" becomes real. Once Agape both shows a fan the ad and processes the card that pays for the meal, it can prove which ad made which sale, the closed loop advertisers pay the most for.

The money line: food → wallet → marketplace. Every fan who orders once has an account and a way to pay inside Agape. Credits, sponsor points and refunds as balance are already stored value. Every stand, runner and local business is a payee Agape pays out. A marketplace that opens with money and payees already inside skips the hardest part of starting one.

The attention line: food → social → advertising and the marketplace. Fans are already sorted by team, section and the friends they came with. Social keeps them in Agape between events, turning an app fans open on event nights into one they open every day. Daily attention is what advertisers buy; buyers already there are what a marketplace needs.

The venue line: runners and venue tools. Payouts, stand alerts, the night's console and its books mean the venue runs its night on Agape, which makes Agape harder to remove than any app a fan could delete. The runner crew is a physical network inside the building: merch drops, seat upgrades and anything else that has to reach a seat can ride on it.

The sponsor line: food → sponsors in the moment. Agape reaches fans mid-purchase, can hand them a sample, a prize or an offer at the seat, then show the sponsor what happened next. That's measured sponsorship rather than a logo on a banner.

Lines the picture leaves out, each real but later: capital for merchants (lending against future card sales, repaid from each day's flow; in late 2025, lending was the main driver of Square's gross profit growth), becoming the processor once volume makes the partner's margin worth taking back, travel and away games, athletes and creators selling straight to the fans who follow them, and new kinds of venue: college and minor-league sports, festivals, theaters and conventions.

6The order of moves

The web gets built in phases that overlap: each starts as soon as the one before has left behind what it needs. Each phase ends at a gate, a thing that must be true before the next phase gets serious money or people.

2. Sponsors and local deals (same nights)Sponsored prizes and samples; offers from places nearby; the "What Agape brought them" report (built)Local businesses see in numbers the customers Agape sent them, and come back for moreProof of customers

Two things keep this from being a wish list. Phases 1 and 2 run on the same nights with the same fans: one launch, not two. And nothing in a later phase is built before its gate is met. A gate that doesn't open is a signal to fix the phase before it, not to skip ahead.

7What could break it, and the defense

RiskWhy it's realThe defense
The concessionaire or venue says noLarge food-service companies and venue register systems have their own apps, and Shift4 owns much of the stadium register business.Agape sells through their stands rather than around them, pays them, and needs nothing installed: new sales on their books, not a competing kitchen.
Ticketing exclusivityPrimary tickets at most venues are locked into long exclusive contracts.The marketplace starts with resale, merch, collectibles, upgrades and local. Primary tickets only through partnership.
Holding fans' moneyBalances and payouts can require money transmitter licenses state by state.Hold money through the processor's accounts until Agape is licensed. Points have no cash value. Counsel before the wallet launches.
The processing partnerThe local line depends on whoever processes.The payee and payout layer is processor-agnostic. Keep a second processor ready; never let one partner own the merchant relationship.
Runners' statusContractor rules differ by state and keep changing.Runners choose their own shifts and trips, paid per delivery plus all tips. Reviewed state by state before each market.
AlcoholDelivering to a seat is regulated, and venues are liable.ID checked at handoff, venue limits enforced, the stand can refuse. Already in the product and terms.
Social never catches onMost social products die empty.Agape's social isn't empty on day one: fans are already grouped by team, section and the friends they came with.
Doing too much too earlyEvery line in the web is tempting.The gates. No phase gets serious money until the one before has opened its gate.

Betting sits next to live events and fan identity, and Fanatics has built a betting business on its fans. For Agape it stays a partnership question at most: heavily regulated, and able to cost the trust of venues, teams and families. It isn't part of the plan.
