## What is Agape?
Food, drinks and gear ordered from your seat at a live event, made by the venue's own kitchens and carried to you by Agape's runners, with an honest time it will arrive. Fans pay Agape; Agape pays the stands, the runners and the tax. From that starting point, Agape becomes the way money moves around live events (see "Where Agape goes").

## Why will fans use it?
Because the line is the worst part of a night out. In Mashgin's 2025 survey, 8 in 10 fans said they've left a concession line without buying, and 79% have missed a key play waiting in one. Oracle found fans would spend 43% more per game if waits were halved. When the Cleveland Cavaliers piloted in-seat ordering, a quarter of fans ordered, at $32 each, and most orders reached the seat within six minutes.

## How does Agape make money?
At events, Agape is the merchant of record: every dollar a fan pays reaches Agape first. Agape keeps a share of each sale plus a delivery fee, then pays the stands, the runners and the tax the same night. Nobody is ever collected from. As it grows, sponsors pay to reach fans in the moment, local businesses pay for the customers Agape sends them and run their card processing through Agape, and advertising and the marketplace follow. The paper walks through each.

## Hasn't this been done?
In-seat and mobile ordering exist, mostly built from the register side: card processors and point-of-sale companies selling to venues (Shift4, for example, bought VenueNext in 2021 and SpotOn's stadium business in 2023). They start with the stand's screen. Agape starts with the fan's moment: an honest arrival time from the live state of every kitchen and runner, runners routed several orders at a time so delivery pays for itself, and every dollar of the night in one set of books. The register can't reach the fan between events; Agape can.

## What's built?
The whole system, working: the fan app, the runner app, the stand's screen, the venue's console, the partner and sponsor view, Agape's own operations, and the books that pay everyone after each night. A venue goes live by filling in a form (its stands, menu, prices and who gets paid), with nothing to install: stands run on a tablet. The demo runs a full arena night (3,000 fans, 40 runners) on the same software a real venue would use, and every role can be tried at agapetoyourseat.com.

## What isn't done yet?
- No venue is live yet. Our first venues are likely in Kentucky.
- Card processing isn't connected yet. The payout side is built so a processor can be swapped in, and the choice between processors is open.
- The terms of use and privacy policy are written to match how Agape works; a lawyer reviews them before the first real night.
- State-by-state rules on alcohol handoff and on runners as independent contractors get checked before each new market.

## What does a venue have to do?
Fill in one form, put a tablet at each stand, and approve who gets paid. Their kitchens make everything at their own prices. Agape brings the orders, the runners and the payments, and pays them after every night. There is no integration project.

## How do runners work?
Runners are independent and choose their own shifts. Each trip shows its pay up front, routes carry several orders at once, and every tip goes to the runner. The system is built around deliveries per runner hour, which is what makes delivery pay at a venue's scale.

## Why start at the seat, if the money is elsewhere?
Because the seat is where Agape earns three things no one else at a venue holds together: the fan in the moment, the money path, and proof of the customers it sends to businesses nearby. Those are what make the next moves (sponsors, local processing, social, the marketplace) possible. "Where Agape goes" sets out the order and what has to be true before each.

## What could go wrong?
The concessionaire or venue could say no; ticketing contracts limit primary tickets; holding fans' money can require licenses; the processing partner could disappoint; runner rules vary by state; social products often fail to catch on; and doing too much too early. Each has a defense in the paper, and each later move waits until the one before it has proved itself.
